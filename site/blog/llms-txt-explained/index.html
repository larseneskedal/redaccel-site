llms.txt explained: what it is, who actually reads it, and whether it is worth ten minutes

Updated November 2026 8 min read By Redaccel
Extract redaccel.com · explainer · November 2026

llms.txt is a proposed Markdown file at your site's root that lists your key pages for AI agents, and the best 2026 evidence says it does not earn AI citations. SISTRIX's study of 96,418 URLs from Q2 2026 found "no measurable effect"[3], and Ahrefs found that 97% of about 38,000 files received no requests at all in May 2026[2]. It has a real use for developer documentation read by coding agents. For most marketing sites it is optional.

What is llms.txt?

It is a Markdown file served at /llms.txt that gives AI agents a short, curated map of a website. Jeremy Howard proposed it on 3 September 2024[1]. It is a proposal, now labelled v2, not a ratified standard, and Google's Chrome documentation calls it an "emerging convention"[8].

The format has a fixed order[1]:

  1. An H1 with the name of the site or project. This is the only required part.
  2. A blockquote with a short summary.
  3. Optional Markdown of any kind except headings, for extra detail.
  4. H2 sections of links, each entry a list item in the form [name](url), with an optional colon and note.
  5. A section called "Optional", by convention, for links an agent can skip when it needs less context.

The proposal also asks sites to offer clean Markdown copies of pages, at the same URL with .md added[1]. Howard's stated expectation was that the file would "mainly be useful for inference rather than training"[1], meaning an agent reads it while helping someone. That detail matters for the verdict below.

It controls nothing. robots.txt is what OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity document for managing their crawlers[10][11][12]. An llms.txt file is a suggested reading list.

Does llms.txt help you get cited by AI engines?

No study we could verify has found that it does. The two largest analyses point the same way, and neither is a controlled test.

  • SISTRIX, published 22 October 2026. The study covers 96,418 URLs from 17,380 domains, using citations from ChatGPT, AI Mode and AI Overviews in Germany, Austria and Switzerland in Q2 2026. Because the file applies to a whole domain, the authors compared domains on this question: 11.7% of cited URLs came from domains with an llms.txt, against 14.0% of all domains. Their words: "As things stand, there is no measurable effect on AI citations"[3]. Perplexity, Claude and the Gemini chat app were not included, and the domains differ in other ways[3].
  • SE Ranking, reported by Search Engine Journal on 20 November 2025. Across about 300,000 domains, 10.13% had the file. SE Ranking found no correlation with citation frequency, and removing the variable from its XGBoost model improved accuracy[4][5]. The write-up names neither the AI engines nor the time window, and gives no accuracy figures[4], so read it as supporting evidence only.

What nobody has published, as far as we could find in November 2026, is a before-and-after test with matched controls, like the one Ahrefs ran on schema that we cover in our notes on page-level LLM optimization. So the honest label is: no evidence of benefit from large correlation studies, and no causal test either way.

Which AI engines actually respect llms.txt?

None of the major engines has committed to using it. Ahrefs' June 2026 study puts it flatly: "no major AI platform has ever committed to reading it"[2]. Google's John Mueller said much the same in April 2025[14]. The table sets each operator's public position beside what logs showed in May 2026.

Google's signals look mixed but are not. Its Search team says to ignore the file, while Lighthouse audits it, which Search Engine Journal documented in May 2026[7]. Asked in January 2026 whether an llms.txt on one of Google's own developer sites was an endorsement, Mueller replied "no"[13]. The same goes for AI companies: OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity all serve llms.txt files on their documentation sites[10][12][16]. Publishing one tells you nothing about whether their engines read yours.

Who is fetching llms.txt files, then?

Mostly SEO tools and other crawlers, with AI agents ahead of AI search bots. Ahrefs studied 137,210 domains on its analytics product. About 38,000 (28%) had a valid file, and "97% of those files received zero traffic in May 2026"[2]. The remaining 3% or so received about 22,000 requests in total, split like this[2]:

  • SEO audit tools: 4,776 requests (21.7%).
  • AI agents and agentic infrastructure: 2,302 (10.5%).
  • AI training crawlers: 1,179 (5.3%).
  • AI assistants: 559 (2.5%).
  • AI retrieval bots: 233 (1.1%).

Retrieval bots are the category most directly tied to live AI answers, and they are the smallest in that list. Ahrefs' reading is that "Agents fetch llms.txt when directed, not speculatively"[2]. Mueller has called the file a "temporary crutch, perhaps to save some tokens", according to the same report[2].

Keep the limits in view. Ahrefs' customers skew technical, so it calls 28% adoption "an upper bound", and a fetch is not proof anyone used the content[2]. It is also one month of data.

Read more: the one dataset that looks more positive

In June 2025 Mintlify, which sells llms.txt generation, published CDN data collected by Profound from 25 companies over 7 days. The median company saw 14 visits to llms.txt and 79 to llms-full.txt[15]. The sample is small, unnamed, older than the Ahrefs data and comes from an interested party, and visits are not usage. Treat it as a sign that some files get regular fetches, not as evidence that they change AI answers.

Should you use an llms.txt generator?

Use one for a first draft, then edit it by hand. Most files on the web already come from templates. Common Crawl analysed 584,107 files from its July 2026 crawl and found 68.27% were template-generated, with Wix alone behind 41.34%[9]. Quality shows it: only 49.90% had the full structure of H1, summary and link sections, and 22.56% contained no links at all[9].

Three checks are worth doing on any generated file:

  • Curate the links. A dump of your sitemap defeats the purpose. List the pages you would send a new colleague to.
  • Remove rules. Common Crawl found policy language the proposal does not define in 6.59% of files, such as rate limits[9]. Access rules belong in robots.txt.
  • Treat it like code. Ahrefs advises version control and restricted editing because of prompt injection risk[2]. Common Crawl flagged 3,793 files with mild steering and 102 promotional ones, as lower bounds from keyword matching[9].

What does our own llms.txt look like?

It follows the proposal loosely, and we can show you where it falls short. Our llms.txt file has been in our site's code since 29 July 2026. Our publishing script rewrites its Articles section each time a post goes out, so that part needs no manual upkeep. The homepage footer links to it, which is the routing Ahrefs recommends[2]. We cannot tell you the file has done anything. This article has no request count for it, because we did not pull one we could verify, and we have no citation result to attribute to it. We keep it because the upkeep is small, and it gives any agent that does look a clean list of pages and our one official domain.

Is llms.txt worth the ten minutes?

Only if you know what you are buying, and for most marketing sites it should not be a priority. SISTRIX reached the same view: "It shouldn't be a priority"[3]. Ahrefs' author concluded "the cons outweigh the pros right now"[2]. Our verdict, which is judgement and not measurement, depends on who reads your site:

  • API, developer tool or documentation site: yes. Coding agents are the one group with visible appetite. Link the file from your docs so agents can find it.
  • Your CMS generates one automatically: leave it on, check it has real links, and spend no more time on it.
  • Marketing, service or ecommerce site: optional. It costs little and carries a small upkeep and security burden. Do it last.
  • Anyone expecting more AI citations from it: no. Nothing published supports that, and Google says Search does not use it[6].

Before deciding, look in your own CDN or server logs for requests to /llms.txt, as Ahrefs suggests[2]. Check IP ranges as well as user agents, because Perplexity, for one, recommends matching both[12]. If your file gets no requests from the bots you care about, you have your answer.

Frequently asked

Does llms.txt work?

As a way to earn AI citations, there is no evidence that it does. SISTRIX found no measurable effect across 96,418 URLs in Q2 2026, and Ahrefs found 97% of about 38,000 files received no requests in May 2026. It does get read by some coding agents, so it can help on developer documentation. Nobody has published a controlled before-and-after test yet.

Does Google use llms.txt?

Google Search does not. Its guide to generative AI features lists llms.txt among things you can ignore and says Google Search itself doesn't use such files, which covers AI Overviews and AI Mode. Chrome's Lighthouse tool does audit the file in its Agentic Browsing category, but that is a developer check, and a missing file simply scores as not applicable.

Is llms.txt the same as robots.txt?

No. robots.txt tells crawlers what they may access, and OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity all document it as the way to manage their bots. llms.txt is a suggested reading list with no access rules in its format. Adding rate limits or denials to it has no defined meaning, even though Common Crawl found that kind of language in 6.59% of files in July 2026.

How do I create an llms.txt file?

Write a Markdown file with your site name as an H1, a short blockquote summary, and H2 sections listing your key pages as links with brief notes. Save it as llms.txt at your site's root and serve it as plain text. A generator can produce a first draft, but edit it by hand, because Common Crawl found 22.56% of files had no links in July 2026.
